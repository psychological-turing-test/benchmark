Large language models can reduce the human effort required for routine judgments. When decisions involve risk and social tradeoffs, however, efficiency alone does not establish whether a model can represent human preferences. Do models choose as people do, and do successive versions converge toward or diverge from human bounded rationality? This tutorial introduces a human-like decision assessment tool to support these questions. Drawing on classic psychological paradigms, it compares choice distributions and responses to changes in decision conditions. Behavioral similarity is assessed relative to specified human references and is distinguished from normative correctness or shared psychological mechanisms.

The tool integrates questionnaire configuration, model access, repeated administration, response validation, and analysis. Choice proportions and condition contrasts provide the primary observations; alignment scores summarize their relationship to human references. A disease-rescue example guides readers through checking materials and answer coding, selecting model settings, running an assessment, inspecting valid counts, and interpreting framing effects. Common materials and analysis rules support procedural comparability, while saved responses permit offline recalculation. The tutorial also explains how to organize later version comparisons by retaining fixed references, recording version identities and settings, and keeping batches separate. Replication through new model calls requires additional checks of distributional stability.

Archived outputs from Chinese-developed DeepSeek, GLM, and Qwen families illustrate the workflow in risky-choice framing and decision-premise tasks. The cross-sectional comparison reveals substantial variation in framing-effect magnitude and task-dependent similarity to human choices. Relative scores can reverse across tasks. Several average decision-premise contrasts are close to zero, but opposing item-level differences can cancel. Constant-choice baselines and sensitivity analyses show why scores must be read alongside response distributions and item composition. Independent recounting and score recalculation support the consistency of the archived analysis. These findings identify questions for further study; they do not establish whether model development is moving toward or away from human behavior.

The tutorial provides a reusable method for examining preferences relevant to the delegation of judgment. Its present conclusions remain conditional on the administered materials, human references, and archived settings. Item-level correspondence to original human studies, omitted respondent-age information, and incomplete historical metadata require further verification. Future studies should validate manipulations, use independent materials, and repeat assessments across documented versions. The tool supports systematic behavioral comparison and subsequent tracking, while decisions about practical use still require evidence appropriate to the intended task and population.
