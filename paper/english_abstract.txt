Large language models can reduce the human effort required for routine judgments. When decisions involve risk and social tradeoffs, however, efficiency alone does not establish whether a model can represent human preferences. Do models choose as people do, and do successive versions converge toward or diverge from human bounded rationality? This tutorial introduces a human-like decision assessment tool for psychological researchers. It compares choice distributions and condition effects with specified human references, distinguishing behavioral similarity from normative correctness and shared psychological mechanisms.

A disease-rescue problem guides readers from research questions to study design and interpretation. The tutorial explains how to select reference populations, check outcome equivalence and manipulations, distinguish repeated model responses from human participants, and plan repetitions around estimation precision. Worked results illustrate why a framing effect in the human direction may coexist with different absolute choice probabilities. Design guidance connects these observations to matched social-cue comparisons, novel materials, and documented model versions. A reporting example shows how to describe choices, contrasts, and uncertainty without extending conclusions beyond the design. Software commands and complete scoring rules are provided in appendices.

Archived outputs from Chinese-developed DeepSeek, GLM, and Qwen families illustrate the approach in risky-choice framing and decision-premise tasks. The cross-sectional comparison reveals substantial variation in framing-effect magnitude and task-dependent similarity to human choices. Relative scores can reverse across tasks. Several average decision-premise contrasts are close to zero, but opposing item-level differences can cancel. Constant-choice baselines and sensitivity analyses demonstrate why scores must be interpreted alongside distributions and item composition. Independent recounting and score recalculation support consistency of the archived analysis. These findings generate further research questions; they do not establish a developmental trend toward or away from human behavior.

The tutorial offers a reusable workflow for studying preferences relevant to the delegation of judgment. Conclusions remain conditional on the administered materials, human references, and archived settings. Item-level correspondence to original human studies, omitted respondent-age information, and incomplete historical records require further verification. Future research should validate manipulations, obtain human comparisons for new materials, and assess stability across batches and documented versions. Applied use requires validation with the intended population and decision context; similarity on these tasks alone does not justify replacing human judgment.
